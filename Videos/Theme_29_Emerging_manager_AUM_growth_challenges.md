# Emerging Manager AUM Growth Challenges

## The Core Problem
The asset management industry is characterized by an extreme concentration of capital. A vast majority of Assets Under Management (AUM) is controlled by a tiny fraction of mega-funds, leaving thousands of emerging managers to fight for a disproportionately small slice of the market. As a result, attrition is incredibly high—most emerging funds fail within their first five years, and the vast majority never cross the crucial $100 million AUM threshold.

## Key Challenges Facing Emerging Managers

### 1. Institutional "Physics" and Constraints
The structural requirements of large institutional allocators (such as pensions and endowments) naturally exclude emerging managers. Institutions often have minimum check sizes (e.g., $20M–$50M) that are mathematically incompatible with a $30M–$60M emerging fund due to concentration limits. Additionally, institutions typically require multi-year track records and avoid first-time funds, effectively barring emerging managers from a massive pool of capital.

### 2. Misaligned Sales and Marketing
Emerging managers frequently misunderstand the nature of capital raising, approaching it as a purely analytical exercise rather than a relationship-building one:
- **Barking Up the Wrong Tree:** Managers waste time marketing to massive institutions that structurally cannot invest in them, rather than targeting appropriately sized family offices or High-Net-Worth Individuals (HNWIs).
- **Leading with Performance Over Trust:** Managers often lead their pitches with tear sheets and performance numbers, skipping the vital steps of establishing their identity, vision, and rapport. They forget that "people buy people."
- **Lack of Scalable Processes:** Marketing efforts are often ad-hoc rather than structured, automated, or scalable. They fail to effectively leverage modern communication tools and media to reach their target audience.

### 3. Working *In* the Business vs. *On* the Business
Many emerging managers are brilliant investors but lack experience in running a business. They spend their time obsessing over their portfolios while neglecting essential business development, compliance, operations, and long-term strategic planning. A lack of a formalized, proactive due diligence process often leaves them scrambling to answer allocator questions instead of leading with transparency.

### 4. The Follow-On Capital Gap
Emerging managers in venture capital are highly adept at identifying early-stage outliers. However, because their LP base often consists of individuals writing smaller checks, these managers lack the dry powder necessary to participate in subsequent financing rounds (Series A, B, C) for their winners. As a result, their capital stops at the seed stage, and they are forced to surrender the majority of the long-term value creation to larger, later-stage funds.

## Potential Solutions & Strategies

- **The "Maven" Approach:** Managers must transition from a transactional sales pitch to a consultative approach. By positioning themselves as trusted experts ("mavens") who solve specific portfolio problems for allocators, they can build deep, trust-based relationships.
- **Proactive Operations:** Establishing bulletproof, scalable operational and due diligence processes is critical. Providing complete transparency to allocators *before* they ask builds institutional credibility.
- **Capital Extension Partnerships:** To solve the follow-on capital gap, emerging managers can leverage capital extension programs or Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs) funded by third-party partners. This allows them to participate in later-stage rounds for their breakout companies, capturing more upside and punching above their weight class.
