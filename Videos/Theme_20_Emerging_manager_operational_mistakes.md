# Analysis: Emerging Manager Operational Mistakes

## Overview
Emerging fund managers often face significant hurdles when scaling their businesses, with a large majority failing to surpass the $100 million AUM mark. The transcripts reveal that these failures are rarely due to poor investment acumen or lack of talent. Instead, they stem from critical operational, structural, and strategic mistakes in how the firm is managed as a business.

## Key Operational Mistakes

### 1. Misunderstanding the Business Model and Target Audience
A fundamental mistake emerging managers make is not clearly defining the specific type of B2B business they are in. They often waste resources and effort pitching to massive institutions (such as large endowments or multi-billion-dollar fund of funds) that structurally cannot allocate to them, instead of identifying and targeting the right LP archetype (e.g., family offices or specialized investors). 

### 2. Lack of a Replicable, Automated Sales Process
Many emerging managers approach fundraising and sales haphazardly. They walk into meetings without a scalable, disciplined process, often immediately presenting performance numbers before establishing rapport, trust, or an understanding of the LP's needs. Furthermore, they fail to leverage modern platforms and automated processes (such as consistent LinkedIn outreach, newsletters, and video content) to build a consistent "sales machine" that keeps the pipeline warm.

### 3. Neglecting Trust-Building and the "Maven" Persona
A major shortfall is forgetting that "people buy people." Managers often hide behind pitch books and tear sheets rather than communicating a unique identity, vision, and core story. Failing to establish themselves as trusted experts (or "mavens") who solve specific problems for allocators makes it difficult to stand out among thousands of competing funds.

### 4. Poor Due Diligence Preparedness
On the compliance and operational side, emerging managers often wait for LPs to request due diligence materials rather than having a comprehensive, bulletproof due diligence manual ready from day one. This reactive approach leads to prolonged diligence cycles—sometimes lasting up to 18 months—and demonstrates a lack of operational maturity. Managers must adopt a proactive "trust but verify" mindset, offering transparency before it is even asked for.

### 5. Absence of a Documented Business Plan
Despite navigating complex regulatory, compliance, and operational costs, many emerging managers lack a formal, documented business plan. Without clear benchmarks, growth trajectories, and accountable milestones for the *business* (separate from the investment portfolio), managers struggle to adapt to market changes or scale their operations efficiently.

### 6. Misaligned Team Dynamics and Conflict Resolution
From an LP's perspective, the greatest operational and existential risk for a new fund is team implosion. First-time teams often launch without having tested their conflict resolution mechanisms. When early investments underperform or the fundraising environment tightens, disagreements can quickly fracture the team. LPs look closely for proven team cohesion, alignment of incentives, and the ability to disagree constructively without derailing the firm.

## Conclusion
To succeed and reach critical scale, emerging managers must transition from merely working *in* their business to working *on* their business. This requires formalizing operations, building a targeted and automated sales funnel, cultivating a distinct public identity, maintaining rigorous due diligence readiness, and ensuring robust team dynamics capable of weathering inevitable setbacks.
