# Venture Capital Research: First-Time Fund Manager Mistakes & Emerging GP Errors

---

## Executive Synthesis & Key Insights

Launching and operating an emerging venture capital fund is fundamentally an exercise in franchise building, institutional stewardship, and probability-weighted capital allocation. However, first-time General Partners (GPs) frequently suffer from severe failure modes. These pitfalls rarely stem from an inability to spot raw talent; rather, they arise from treating fund management as an informal extension of angel investing rather than a disciplined financial system governed by power-law dynamics.

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|               FIRST-TIME VC FUND MANAGER FAILURE MODES & ROOT CAUSES              |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                                                                                   |
|  1. Portfolio Construction & Sizing Errors                                        |
|     • Fund sizing driven by ego rather than strategy mechanics                    |
|     • Logo collecting over meaningful ownership (<5% vs. fund-returning stakes)   |
|     • Ad-hoc reserve allocation ("vibes") starving breakout winners               |
|                                                                                   |
|  2. LP Relationship & Fundraising Missteps                                        |
|     • Prohibitive LP minimum check sizes alienating high-value strategic LPs      |
|     • Going dark during portfolio downturns / J-curve distress                    |
|     • Misunderstanding institutional LP diligence cycles & underwriting hurdles   |
|                                                                                   |
|  3. Decision-Making Biases & Power-Law Violations                                 |
|     • Acute loss aversion during Fund I fundraising (fearing early shutdowns)     |
|     • Asking "Why will this fail?" instead of "What if this works 100x?"          |
|     • Valuation rigidity on generational outlier companies                        |
|                                                                                   |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

### 1. Portfolio Construction and Sizing Mismatches
A ubiquitous error among first-time GPs is raising a fund size that flatters their ego rather than one engineered for their strategy. An oversized Fund I forces managers to write larger checks and compete directly with established brand-name venture firms without the requisite brand equity or value proposition to win allocation. Conversely, new GPs often dilute their potential returns by "collecting logos"—writing small, non-lead checks in competitive rounds without securing enough target ownership (often 7–15% at seed) for a winning company to return the fund. Furthermore, emerging managers often manage reserves reactively, either neglecting pro-rata reserves entirely or throwing good money after bad to rescue stagnant investments instead of doubling and tripling down on breakout winners.

### 2. LP Relationship and Communication Traps
First-time managers frequently misunderstand the psychology of Limited Partners (LPs). GPs often set prohibitively high minimum check sizes ($250k–$500k+), inadvertently alienating strategic angel-sized LPs, operators, and founders who provide proprietary deal flow, diligence assistance, and warm introductions to institutional allocators. Furthermore, when portfolio challenges or negative marks emerge (standard during the 3–5 year venture J-curve), inexperienced managers often reduce reporting frequency or go silent. In reality, institutional LPs underwrite managerial integrity and intellectual honesty; transparent, cadence-driven communication during adverse cycles cements long-term trust and ensures re-up commitments for Funds II and III.

### 3. Cognitive Biases and Asymmetric Underwriting
Because first-time GPs are actively pitching LPs while deploying their initial capital, they fall prey to acute loss aversion. Fearing that an early portfolio company shutdown will derail their fundraising narrative, managers pass on unconventional, high-variance founders (e.g., Meesho, Bitcoin, Sorare) with short cash runways or unproven business models. This violates the core tenet of early-stage venture: downside is capped at 1x, while upside follows a power-law distribution exceeding 100x–500x. Emerging GPs routinely ask "Why won't this work?" rather than "If this works, what can it become?" By over-indexing on downside risk and entry valuation multiples on outlier companies, emerging managers commit catastrophic errors of omission.

### Article 1: Top Mistakes First-Time Fund Managers Make

* **Source:** *Signature Block* (by Weekend Fund)
* **Authors:** Ryan Hoover & Vedika Jain
* **Contributors:** Beezer Clarkson (Sapphire Partners), Bubba Murarka (Eleven Prime), Courtney Reum (M13), Harry Stebbings (20VC), Kyle Samani (Multicoin Capital), Nikhil Basu Trivedi (Footwork), Terrence Rohan (Otherwise Fund)
* **URL:** [https://www.signatureblock.co/articles/top-mistakes-first-time-fund-managers-make](https://www.signatureblock.co/articles/top-mistakes-first-time-fund-managers-make)

#### Article Overview & Key Lessons
Knowing what not to do is just as critical when starting something new. In this post, nine experienced GPs and seasoned LPs share the biggest mistakes they see first-time fund managers make, accompanied by actionable lessons to help emerging managers build successful, durable franchises.

Much of this advice is subjective. There are many ways to play the VC game and another GP’s strategy may not match your own. Managers should avoid blindly copying another fund’s approach and instead double down on their unique differentiation.

#### TL;DR of Ten Core Lessons:
1. Have a clear long-term vision for the firm you’re building.
2. Align your fund size with your fund strategy.
3. Design a portfolio construction plan that enables you to deliver on your fund strategy.
4. Get comfortable with LP timelines (and lots of no’s!).
5. Have (or secure over time) enough ownership in winners to return the fund.
6. Double down, triple down (or more) on winners.
7. Avoid pricing out strategic, helpful LPs with high check size minimums.
8. Don’t pass on an investment because it looks “strange”.
9. Communicate clearly and consistently with LPs — they’re the ones that keep you in business.
10. Never count a portfolio company out.

---

#### Full Text Content:

##### 1. Long-Term Vision
**Lesson:** As a fund manager, you are also a firm builder. Having a clear long-term vision for the firm you’re building guides strategic decisions like your investment focus, fund size, team, and more. Of course, this vision will evolve as you invest, learn more about the kind of investor you want to be, and what’s working through feedback from founders, LPs, and the broader market.

##### 2. Audience Building
**Lesson:** Writing and content creation can be an extremely effective way for first-time fund managers to attract the founders, companies, and co-investors they want to attract. Building an authentic voice in public helps establish proprietary deal flow and brand recognition before reaching institutional scale.

##### 3. Minimum Check Sizes for LPs
**Lesson:** Smaller LPs can be extremely helpful with sharing deal flow, helping portfolio founders, co-investing in opportunities, and more. Furthermore, warm LP intros from people that are investing in your fund are often the best kind of intros. An LP writing a $10K check might introduce you to an LP that writes a $1M check. Many fund managers instrument high minimums to avoid exceeding a 99 LP cap, but there are structural ways (such as fund structures and feeder vehicles) to manage larger numbers of supportive LPs.

##### 4. Objectives with LPs
**Lesson:** Like GPs, LPs have limited time and receive a flood of inbound requests from those raising, so be direct about your objectives when reaching out to LPs. If you want to pitch them for a raise, say so. If you want to start building a relationship for future raises, say so clearly up front.

##### 5. Institutional LP Timelines (and Lots of No’s!)
**Lesson:** While there are tactical ways to create urgency with LPs, it is essential to be respectful of institutional LP timelines and know your audience. Institutional allocators operate on annual pacing schedules, committee reviews, and multi-meeting diligence processes. First-time GPs must budget for long sales cycles and develop thick skin for hundreds of rejections.

##### 6. Misaligned Fund Size with Fund Strategy
**Lesson:** Align your fund size with your fund strategy. A larger AUM is appealing and provides higher management fees, but there are major structural tradeoffs. Fund size informs the number of deals, target check sizes, reserve allocations, ownership requirements, and required exit sizes. Sizing a fund beyond your deployment capability or strategy breaks the underlying return model.

##### 7. Ownership in Winners
**Lesson:** Be thoughtful about the trade-off between ownership and squeezing into competitive deals. Ideally, every company you back has the mathematical potential to return the fund (or more), although this isn’t a blanket “rule” and ultimately ownership targets should be established in your portfolio construction model.

##### 8. Doubling Down on Winners
**Lesson:** Buying (or maintaining) ownership by writing multiple checks over the life of a company can have a major impact on the fund’s performance. As an existing investor, you have proprietary insight into company execution, giving you an informational advantage in spotting portfolio breakouts. Securing pro-rata rights during initial checks is critical, but managers must also earn the right to invest in subsequent rounds by providing genuine value to founders.

##### 9. Portfolio Construction
**Lesson:** Have a clear, deliberate portfolio construction plan that enables you to deliver on your fund strategy. Portfolio construction involves multiple interdependent variables—deal count, check size variance, follow-on reserves, fee drag, and recycling—that must be modeled out with realistic, stress-tested assumptions.

##### 10. LP Updates
**Lesson:** GPs must keep their LPs informed on how capital is being deployed. Clear, consistent LP updates establish trust and encourage LPs to commit to consecutive funds. Furthermore, high-quality updates turn LPs into active partners who can assist with customer introductions, follow-on syndication, and candidate hiring.

##### 11. Counting a Founder Out
**Lesson:** Support every portfolio company through operational difficulty. Turnarounds and pivots happen frequently; disengaging prematurely damages GP reputation and impairs the manager's ability to participate in eventual breakout pivots.

##### 12. "Strange" Investments
**Lesson:** Breakthrough innovation is inherently non-consensus and frequently looks strange or unviable at inception. When evaluating companies, rather than finding reasons why an idea will fail, GPs should ask: *"What if this works? How big can it become?"*

##### 13. Investing Without Social Proof
**Lesson:** While social proof and co-investor pedigree can provide validation, successful fund managers must have the conviction to invest without consensus validation from brand-name firms, especially when leading rounds.

##### 14. Sourcing Strategy to Lead Deals
**Lesson:** Leading rounds is a zero-sum competition for a single lead investor spot. Relying purely on passive inbound deal flow magnifies adverse selection. Emerging managers looking to lead must build active outbound sourcing mechanisms, proprietary network maps, and specialized domain advantages.

##### 15. First Close Execution
**Lesson:** Execute a first close to start deploying capital and building a track record. Holding multiple closes creates rolling momentum and nudges hesitant LPs to commit before final subscription deadlines.

### Article 2: Learnings from Passing on Unicorns: VCs Share Their Big Misses, Why They Passed and Lessons Learned

* **Source:** *Signature Block* (by Weekend Fund)
* **Authors:** Ryan Hoover & Vedika Jain
* **Contributors:** Nicholas Chirls (Notation Capital), AJ Solimine & Evan Tana (Script Capital), Elizabeth Yin (Hustle Fund), Bessemer Venture Partners
* **URL:** [https://www.signatureblock.co/articles/learnings-from-passing-on-unicorns](https://www.signatureblock.co/articles/learnings-from-passing-on-unicorns)

#### Article Overview
Venture capital operates under extreme power-law returns where missing a single breakout outlier can cost hundreds of millions in fund returns. The most fatal mistakes in venture investing are *errors of omission* rather than *errors of commission*. In this piece, top venture capitalists examine their biggest misses, analyze their psychological blind spots, and share lessons to improve future investment decision-making.

#### Four Main Takeaways:
1. **Have an open mind:** Breakthrough innovation rarely fits neat historical patterns; it often looks weird and defies early pattern-matching.
2. **Ask "Why will this work?":** Deep domain expertise often leads investors to identify every possible point of failure rather than envisioning outlier success.
3. **Ask "If this works, what can this become?":** Category-defining companies create and expand markets through second-order opportunities over time.
4. **Entry valuations are relative to return potential:** Passing on transformative companies because initial seed/Series A valuations look "rich" is a recurring error.

---

#### Case Studies & Full Text Content:

##### Case 1: Nicholas Chirls (Notation Capital) — Passing on Headway
* **Miss:** Headway (mental health marketplace and provider network; subsequent valuation $750M+, backed by Andreessen Horowitz).
* **The Error:** Categorizing Headway as just another mental health directory in a crowded market.
* **The GP Reflection:**
> *"A pass that really hurts for me is Headway. We passed on the pre-seed round and I think about it all the time because the company has the potential to be a category-defining company in NYC. I passed at the time mainly because there were a few similar mental health provider search products in the market, and I wrongly bucketed them in the same camp. Instead, they eventually chose to build out a provider network to help providers manage insurance payments, which proved to be a much better model, both for patients to find in-network care, and a much better business for Headway. My main learning here is to always find ways to keep an open mind, even in what are seemingly competitive spaces and look for innovative approaches and business models that can stand out above the rest."*

##### Case 2: AJ Solimine & Evan Tana (Script Capital) — Passing on Meesho
* **Miss:** Meesho (social commerce platform in India; grew from pre-seed to a $5B+ valuation, representing a ~500x return miss).
* **The Error:** Loss aversion and fear of portfolio shutdowns during active Fund I fundraising.
* **The GP Reflection:**
> *"Evan and I have been interested in WhatsApp as a general commerce platform for about as long as we can remember. So in early 2017 when a friend sent us the concept for Meesho, we jumped at the opportunity to meet. We were convinced there was a huge opportunity and Vidit was special, so we committed to invest.*
>
> *We were a few months into fundraising for our first fund, and a big part of pitching LPs involves showing off the existing portfolio so new LPs know what they’re buying into. Meesho would be one of our first 3 investments out of the fund.*
>
> *Naturally, we were concerned about runway to ensure we wouldn’t have to pitch LPs with any shutdowns already in our portfolio. So before closing the investment, we asked Meesho for recent financials and marketing spend and realized that with their current spend, they were running on a very tight runway. We got spooked. We told Vidit that based on the limited runway, we couldn’t get comfortable making the investment.*
>
> *On a micro level — it made sense to avoid potential shutdowns in the portfolio while the fund was getting off the ground and we were still pitching LPs. But that conservative strategy led us to miss an investment that could ~500x+ our original investment. As young VCs we often suffered from Loss Aversion... We were so afraid of a quick potential shutdown that we missed what we knew could be a 1,000x return."*

##### Case 3: Elizabeth Yin (Hustle Fund) — Passing on Bitcoin
* **Miss:** Bitcoin in 2011 at ~$0.50 (a 40,000x return asymmetry).
* **The Error:** Inability to underwrite extreme asymmetric distribution tails without clear precedent.
* **The GP Reflection:**
> *"Honestly, my biggest miss was probably Bitcoin. I learned about it in early 2011 or so, and I think it was about 50 cents... While this sounds laughable, I think that one of my biggest learnings is that sometimes it makes sense to just take a small flyer on many things. There are many investments that are accessible to the general public, including cryptocurrency, and you never really know where anything is going to go. One way to hedge against extreme unpredictability is through a portfolio strategy by placing smaller bets on innovations with extreme outcome distributions."*

##### Case 4: Ryan Hoover & Vedika Jain (Weekend Fund) — Passing on Sorare
* **Miss:** Sorare (fantasy sports & digital collectables platform; seed pass in April 2020 followed 18 months later by a $680M round at a $4.3B valuation — a 250x valuation surge).
* **The Error:** Doubting scalability beyond initial fanatic users and valuation sensitivity.
* **The GP Reflection:**
> *"We were extremely impressed with their traction and the opportunity to sign exclusives with clubs and leagues. After a lot of back and forth, we ended up passing on the company driven by doubts about whether they’ll be able to sustain their growth as they scale beyond crypto-native audiences. Our intuition was that a large percentage of paying users were purely speculating and worried that it would be unsustainable. Combined with a higher valuation than we normally entered at the seed stage at that time, we opted to pass.*
>
> *Learnings:*
> 1. *We underestimated the value of a small but intense group of early adopters. What people are obsessed with holds secrets about what is going to blow up.*
> 2. *We weighed too heavily a reason for why Sorare won’t work rather than why it will work.*
> 3. *We over-indexed on entry valuation rather than long-term market expansion."*

##### Case 5: Bessemer Venture Partners Anti-Portfolio — Valuation Traps (Airbnb, Apple, Atlassian)
* **Misses:**
  * **Airbnb:** Jeremy Levine met Brian Chesky in January 2010 ($100K/month revenue). Brian’s $40M valuation ask was deemed "crazy." By April, Airbnb raised at 1.5x the price, and later completed rounds at ~500x that valuation.
  * **Apple:** Passed on pre-IPO secondary stock at a $60M valuation because it was labeled "outrageously expensive."
  * **Atlassian:** Evaluated in 2006 (self-financed, credit card bootstrapped). In 2010, the $400M valuation was deemed "too rich," causing the firm to miss a multi-billion dollar category leader.
* **Core Takeaway:** Outlier companies routinely appear overpriced on trailing multiples because traditional models fail to capture second-order compounding and market expansion.

## Comparative Matrix: First-Time Fund Manager Mistakes vs. Institutional Best Practices

| Domain | First-Time GP Error | Institutional Best Practice |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Fund Sizing** | Raising an unmanageable fund size to maximize fee revenue or social prestige. | Target a "Minimum Viable Fund" calibrated to check size, target ownership, and team capacity. |
| **Portfolio Ownership** | Spraying small non-lead checks (<3%) to accumulate brand logos. | Maintain strict ownership targets (7–15%+) ensuring portfolio winners return the vehicle. |
| **Reserve Management** | Managing reserves ad-hoc or propping up underperforming bridge rounds. | Model disciplined, formulaic follow-on reserves dedicated exclusively to breakout winners. |
| **LP Minimums** | Setting rigid $250k–$500k minimums that repel valuable angel/operator LPs. | Leverage flexible minimums or feeder vehicles to assemble high-conviction strategic advocates. |
| **Downside Framing** | Operating under loss aversion; passing on high-beta deals to avoid Fund I write-downs. | Embrace power-law asymmetry; optimize for 100x+ upside rather than minimizing 1x losses. |
| **LP Communication** | Withholding bad news or reducing cadence during market downturns. | Maintain rigorous quarterly transparency; treat LP reporting as a continuous relationship asset. |
